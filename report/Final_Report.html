Forecasting Weekly Bakery Demand With Gradient Descent: From Store-Level Forecasts to a Reproducible Planning Tool

1. Executive Summary

Problem. A bakery distributor must decide every week how many units of each product to deliver to each store. Too little bread leaves empty shelves and lost sales; too much is returned or thrown away (United Nations Environment Programme [UNEP], 2021). Today these decisions rely on experience and simple averages.

What I built. Using 13.7 million weekly delivery records of the 10 best-selling Grupo Bimbo products (Kaggle, 2016a), I built a forecast with a plain formula of four ingredients: what the store usually orders of the product, the typical level of the product, the typical level of the store, and whether the store-product pair is new. The weights of the formula were learned with Gradient Descent, a step-by-step learning method that I programmed myself. The formula is served by a small forecast service that anyone can run on a laptop.

Main result. On a week the formula never saw during learning (week 9), it explains 69% of the variation in demand, while always guessing the average explains none. The typical error is 4.2 units per store and product, against 7.9 units for the average. Two weeks ahead, the result is almost the same (68%). The forecast is slightly low for every product: the shortfall adds up to 25.7% of demand and the excess to 14.1%, so the main risk is empty shelves.

Recommendation. Use the forecast as the default delivery quantity in a pilot with a few depots, add a safety margin to products where the forecast is low (largest for product 2233, and extra care for the two weakest products, 36610 and 45143), and measure stockouts and returns before and after. A first improvement is to correct the low bias of the forecast.

2. Business Context and Objective

Organization and scenario. The scenario is the distribution planning team of a bakery company that delivers fresh products to stores every week. Bread has a very short shelf life, so a wrong delivery has a direct cost: a lost sale on one side and a write-off on the other. The data come from Grupo Bimbo, a large bakery company that publishes weekly delivery data in a public competition (Kaggle, 2016a).

Table 1

Stakeholders and the Decisions the Forecast Supports

Stakeholder Decision What they gain
Demand planners Delivery quantity for each store and product A default quantity that is more accurate than the simple average
Depot and route managers Loading plan and routes Fewer emergency re-deliveries and fewer returns
Finance Budget for write-offs A way to measure the cost of stale, unsold bread

Objective. Forecast the weekly demand of the 10 best-selling products for every store using only information from earlier weeks, and express the remaining error in units of stockout and excess stock. Success was defined at the start of the project as an R² of at least 0.5 on a week the model never learned, an error clearly below the baseline of always predicting the average, and a model that a planner can explain. These conditions are met (Section 7).

Origins of the method. Gradient Descent comes from the stochastic approximation idea of Robbins and Monro (1951) and is now the standard way to train models on large data, because the mini-batch version learns from one chunk of data at a time (Bottou, 2010; Bottou et al., 2018). In the Grupo Bimbo competition, the winning teams relied on lagged demand and group averages as features (Kaggle, 2016b); this project uses the same idea in a simpler, explainable form.

Value and limits. About 931 million tonnes of food were wasted worldwide in 2019 (UNEP, 2021). Better forecasts are one way to reduce waste of fresh products, but this report does not claim a specific saving: the effect on returns can only be measured in a pilot (Section 9). Connection to the project. The competition asks for next-week demand per store and product, which is exactly the weekly decision of a distribution planner, and the large table makes Gradient Descent a natural choice.

5. Data and Preparation

Source and access. The data come from the Kaggle competition Grupo Bimbo Inventory Demand (Kaggle, 2016a). The training table has 74,180,464 rows (weeks 3-9, with the target) and the test table has weeks 10 and 11 without the target. The identifiers are anonymized, there is no personal data, and the data are used only for this course assignment under the competition terms. One row is the delivery of one product to one store in one week through one depot, route, and sales channel. The target is Adjusted_Demand_Units, the units a store actually needed after returns.

SQL preparation. With 74 million rows, the full table is too large for a normal laptop. Applying the course MSBA 230, I loaded both files into SQL Server, renamed the Spanish column names to English, checked every column for NULL and negative values (none were found), and selected the 10 best-selling products: those with the largest total adjusted demand in weeks 3-8 that were sold in every training week and appear in both test weeks. The result is two tables, train_filtered (13,721,629 rows) and test_filtered (169,578 rows). All of their rows are used; nothing is sampled. For reproducibility, the two tables are stored in the repository as Parquet files, and one script loads them into any SQL Server and checks the row counts, so a reviewer does not need the Kaggle download.

Preparation decisions.

  • Skewed target. Demand is strongly right-skewed (skewness 24.1; median 5 units, 99th percentile 80, maximum 3,850). After log(1 + x) the skewness is 0.54, within the course limit of 1 (Solis, 2026c), so the model predicts log(1 + demand) and the result is converted back to units.

  • No leakage. The test table has only identifiers and the week, so the sales of the forecast week cannot be used (they correlate 0.997 with demand). Every feature is calculated only from earlier weeks.

  • Extreme values. Demand above the 99.9th percentile (175 units) is capped in the training rows only, never in validation or test.

  • New stores and products. They have no history, so they receive the overall average and a flag set to 1.

  • Large data. Each week is read from SQL in chunks of at most one million rows, so memory use stays small.

Figure 1

Distribution of the Target (Log Scale) and Share of Demand by Product

Note. Left: histogram of log(1 + demand). Right: share of total demand by product.

Figure 1 shows that the log transformation turns a long right tail into a roughly bell-shaped distribution, which suits a linear model, and that a few products carry a large share of the volume, so accuracy on those products matters most for the business.

Data limits. Only seven weeks of history exist, so seasonality cannot be learned; the test labels are hidden, so a true test score cannot be calculated; and there is no information on promotions, weather, or holidays that could explain demand changes.

6. Method and Implementation

6.1 Features Built Only From Earlier Weeks

I designed 22 candidate features that summarize earlier weeks: smoothed average demand of the product, store, depot, route, channel, and store-product pair (the target-encoding idea of Micci-Barreca, 2001; Solis, 2026d), popularity counts, the last orders of the pair, weeks since the last order, return rates, price, and flags for stores or products never seen before. Appendix B lists all 22.

6.2 Selecting Features Like a Best-Fit Model in JMP

Duplicate features (|r| ≥ 0.85) were merged (22 to 18). Features then entered the equation one at a time, always the one with the largest gain in R²; the table of each step records the intercept, the coefficient, R², and the mean squared error (MSE); and features that add less than 0.002 in R² were dropped. Four features remain (Table 5).

Table 5

The Four Features in the Final Equation

Feature Meaning How it is calculated
mean3_cust_prod Average of the store's last three orders of this product Mean of log(1 + demand) in the three latest earlier weeks in which the pair was ordered; if fewer exist, the latest is repeated; if the pair is new, the product's level
te_product Typical demand level of the product (Sum of log(1 + demand) of the product's earlier rows + 5 × G) / (number of those rows + 5), where G is the overall average
te_customer Typical demand level of the store Same formula over the store's earlier rows
is_new_cust_prod The store never ordered this product before 1 if the pair has no earlier row, otherwise 0

6.3 Training With Gradient Descent

Model: multiple linear regression trained with mini-batch Gradient Descent, written from scratch in NumPy (Harris et al., 2020), following the two training strategies of the course, the Normal Equation and Gradient Descent (Solis, 2026e). Features are standardized during learning and the weights are converted back to the original scale. Training uses an expanding window: for each week from 4 to 8, the features come only from earlier weeks, the week is first scored (an honest out-of-sample R²) and then used for learning, and the weights are carried to the next week. Week 9 is held out for validation. The exact least-squares solution is computed as a cross-check.

Table 6

Gradient Descent Settings

Setting Value
Learning rate 0.05
Epochs per chunk 10
Mini-batch size 4096
L2 penalty 0
Maximum rows per chunk 1,000,000
Random seed 42

Baseline and metrics. The baseline always predicts the average of the training weeks. Results use R² and RMSLE on the log scale (RMSLE is the competition metric), and MAE and RMSE in units. The business effect is shown as the share of demand under-forecast (stockout risk) and over-forecast (excess-stock risk).

7. Evaluation and Findings

R² on the log scale is the main metric because it shows how much of the variation in demand the formula explains, independent of the units. It is compared on the same validation rows with the baseline and with the exact least-squares solution, which tests whether Gradient Descent converged.

Table 8

Results on Validation Week 9, Which the Model Never Learned

Metric Gradient Descent Baseline (always the mean) Exact least squares
R² (log scale) 0.691 -0.001 0.685
RMSLE (lower is better) 0.510 0.917 0.514
R² (units) 0.642 -0.050 0.642
MAE (units) 4.17 7.90 4.25
RMSE (units) 11.19 19.16 11.19

Note. Two weeks ahead (simulated on week 9 with history one week older): R² = 0.681, RMSLE = 0.518.

Table 8 shows that the formula explains about 69% of the variation of log demand, while the baseline explains none, and the typical error falls from 7.9 to 4.2 units. Gradient Descent and the exact solution agree (0.691 and 0.685), so the optimization converged. Training R² is 0.656, slightly below the validation value, so the model does not overfit. Before learning each training week, the out-of-sample R² was 0.650, 0.667, 0.679, and 0.686 for weeks 5 to 8, rising as the history grows.

Figure 3

R² and MSE as Each Feature Enters the Equation

Note. The dotted line marks the final model of four features.

Figure 3 and Table 9 show that the first feature, the store's last three orders of the product, already explains most of the variation, and that after the fourth feature the curve is flat: the other 14 features together add less than 0.01 in R². This is why the final model is small and can be explained to a planner.

Final equation (original feature scale):

log(1 + demand) = -0.657 + 0.648 * mean3_cust_prod + 0.345 * te_product

+ 0.329 * te_customer - 0.340 * is_new_cust_prod

demand (units) = exp( log(1 + demand) ) - 1

The standardized weights show the same order of importance: 0.53 for the store's last orders, 0.19 for the product level, 0.11 for the store level, and −0.12 for a new pair. A store-product pair that never ordered before lowers the forecast by 0.340 on the log scale, the price of having no history.

8. Advanced Component and Reproducibility

8.1 Saved and Reloaded Model

The training notebook saves the fitted weights with their scaling (model_final.npz), the history statistics that rebuild the features (history_stats_final.pkl), the validation metrics, and a set of 2,000 stored predictions (reference_check.npz). The script load_and_test_demo.py starts a fresh program without any database, loads these files, and (a) checks that the reloaded model reproduces the 2,000 stored predictions (printed as PASS or FAIL) and (b) forecasts example stores, including one customer that the model has never seen, which receives the product's average instead of an error. The input is a Customer_ID and a Product_ID; the output is the forecast in units. The history file is large (about 200 MB), so it stays out of the repository and is recreated by the training notebook.

8.2 Forecast API (FastAPI Extension)

For the optional FastAPI extension, the frozen equation is served by a small local API. A build script keeps only the lookup tables that the four features need, which gives two artifact files of 13 MB in total (api/artifacts/) that are stored in the repository and need only NumPy, so the API runs right after cloning without SQL Server or training.

Table 12

Endpoints of the Forecast API

Endpoint Input Output
GET /health - {"status": "ready", "model_id": "..."}
POST /predict {"customer_id": 1502792, "product_id": 1242} predicted_units, predicted_log_demand, the four feature values, and flags that show whether the store, product, or pair was known

Invalid input (for example, a negative customer number or a missing field) returns a clear validation error (HTTP 422) instead of a forecast. The script smoke_check_api.py reads the reference stored when the model was frozen, calls the health endpoint, sends the example request, and compares the answer with the reference (tolerance 10⁻⁶ on the log scale); it prints PASS when both match. The reference was computed with the full original pipeline, and an additional comparison on 3,320 store-product pairs, including unseen stores and products, gave a largest difference of 3.1 × 10⁻⁷ on the log scale. For the example store and product, the API returns 5.7 units.

8.3 How to Reproduce the Results

The README of the repository lists the steps in order: create the Python environment and install requirements.txt; create an empty SQL Server database and load the two Parquet files with one script that checks the row counts; test the connection; run the training notebook, then the test notebook; run the load-and-test script; and start the API and run the smoke check. Every step states the output that must appear. Library requirements are in requirements.txt, Python 3.14.6, numpy 2.5.4, pandas 3.1.0

8.4 Independent Reproduction by a Classmate

Wendy cloned the repository on Nov 1 (Part1) and Nov 17 (Part 2) and followed the README without my help. The classmate reproduced the data load and the training, and obtained the same expected scores for the test weeks (R² 0.6906 and 0.6808; RMSLE 0.5097 and 0.5178 against my 0.5098 and 0.5177, a difference in the fourth decimal that comes from the computer). The load-and-test check printed PASS. Because the classmate trained the model again on another computer, the intercept was −0.6717 instead of −0.6570: Gradient Descent on millions of rows follows a slightly different path on another machine, and the correlated features trade weight with the intercept, but the forecasts stay almost identical.

The API smoke check printed PASS on the validator’s computer. The reproduction also exposed three problems, which I fixed:

  1. Data distribution. At first I shared the filtered train and test tables as two CSV files on Google Drive, because GitHub would not accept them and downloading the original data from Kaggle would take the validator a long time to clean, translate into English, and filter in SQL. The CSV export from SQL could not hold all the rows because of the row limit of the spreadsheet program. I changed the format to Parquet. The two files now fit in the repository, so Google Drive is no longer needed, and the import script checks that the row counts match (13,721,629 and 169,578).

  2. SQL connection. On the validator’s computer, the import script could not load the data into SQL Server. I changed the script import_parquet_to_sql.py to open the connection with engine.begin(), so that the loading works on another computer as well.

  3. Slightly different values after training. The R² and the intercept printed after training were not identical to mine. The differences were small (the fourth decimal of the expected R² and RMSLE, and the intercept, −0.6717 against −0.6570), and the classmate’s forecasts were almost identical to mine. Gradient Descent on millions of rows follows a slightly different path on another computer because of small differences in the calculation, so a tiny difference in the last digits is expected and does not change the conclusions. I added a note about this to the README.

9. Recommendation, Limitations, and Next Steps

9.1 Recommendation

  • Run a pilot in a few depots: use the forecast as the default delivery quantity and let planners override it. Track stockouts and returns for the pilot depots and for similar depots without the forecast, so that the effect can be measured and not assumed.

  • Add a safety margin per product because every product is forecast low. A reasonable starting point is the shortfall seen in week 9 (Table 10), for example about 26% for product 2233 and 16% for product 1250, to be tuned during the pilot.

  • Treat products 36610 and 45143 with extra care. Their orders are large and variable and the formula explains little of them (R² 0.14 and 0.19), so planners should review these forecasts by hand.

  • Refresh the forecast every week by adding the new sales to the history, which also updates the four features.

9.2 Limitations and Risks

  • The model is linear and uses four features, so it cannot capture promotions, holidays, or seasonality; only seven weeks of history exist.

  • The forecast is biased low (25.7% of demand under-forecast against 14.1% over-forecast); a correction has not been implemented yet.

  • New store-product pairs (5-6% of the test rows) only receive an average-based forecast, and the true test score cannot be known because the labels are hidden; the expected scores are estimates from week 9.

  • Only the 10 best-selling products were modeled, so the result should not be applied to slow-selling products without testing. The data contain no personal information, and the identifiers are anonymized; the forecast is about products, not about people.

9.3 Next Steps

  • Correct the downward bias of the conversion from the log scale to units (a smearing factor estimated on week 9) and report the totals before and after.

  • Test further history features (for example, demand two to three weeks back) with the same feature ladder; the ladder is already flat after four features, so large gains are not expected from a linear model.

  • Design the pilot and the weekly feedback loop shown as planned steps in Figure 2, and compare returns before and after.

The 22 Candidate Features

Table A1

Candidate Features: Type, Meaning, and Calculation

Feature Type Meaning Calculation
te_product Average Typical demand level of the product (sum of log1p(demand) of the product's earlier rows + m*G) / (number of those rows + m)
te_customer Average Typical demand level of the store (sum of log1p(demand) of the store's earlier rows + 5·G) / (number of those rows + 5)
te_agency Average Typical demand level of the depot (sum of log1p(demand) of the depot's earlier rows + 5·G) / (number of those rows + 5)
te_route Average Typical demand level of the delivery route (sum of log1p(demand) of the route's earlier rows + 5·G) / (number of those rows + 5)
te_channel Average Typical demand level of the sales channel (sum of log1p(demand) of the channel's earlier rows + 5·G) / (number of those rows + 5)
te_cust_prod Average Typical demand of this store for this product (sum of log1p(demand) of the earlier rows of the store-product pair + 5·G) / (number of those rows + 5); if the pair is new, the product's te_product is used
logcnt_product Count-based How often the product is ordered log(1 + earlier order lines of the product / weeks of history)
logcnt_customer Count-based How often the store orders log(1 + earlier order lines of the store / weeks of history)
logcnt_agency Count-based How busy the depot is log(1 + earlier order lines of the depot / weeks of history)
logcnt_route Count-based How busy the route is log(1 + earlier order lines of the route / weeks of history)
logcnt_channel Count-based How busy the sales channel is log(1 + earlier order lines of the channel / weeks of history)
logcnt_cust_prod Count-based How regularly the store orders this product log(1 + earlier order lines of the store-product pair / weeks of history)
last_cust_prod Earlier order Most recent demand of the store for this product log1p(demand) in the latest earlier week in which the pair was ordered; if new, te_cust_prod
prev_cust_prod Earlier order The order before the most recent one log1p(demand) of the second-latest earlier order of the pair; if missing, last_cust_prod
mean3_cust_prod Earlier order Average of the last three orders mean of last_cust_prod, prev_cust_prod and the third-latest order of the pair
gap_cust_prod Count-based Weeks since the store last ordered this product log(1 + (current week - week the pair was last seen)); 0 if never seen
ret_cust_prod Rate Share of this store's product that came back unsold sqrt of (returned units + 5*p) / (sold units + 5), where p = the product's overall return rate; the square root reduces the right skew
log_hist_price Volume Price level of the product log(1 + historical sales value in pesos / units sold) of the product
hist_return_rate Rate Share of the product that usually comes back unsold historical returned units / units sold of the product (0 to 1)
is_new_customer Flag Store never seen before 1 if the store has no earlier history, otherwise 0
is_new_product Flag Product never seen before 1 if the product has no earlier history, otherwise 0
is_new_cust_prod Flag Store has never ordered this product before 1 if the store-product pair has no earlier history, otherwise 0

Note. All features use only weeks before the row's week. G is the overall average of log(1 + demand) in the history, and the smoothing weight m is 5.
